How it works

The page builds 10,000 possible futures for the BTC price from real price moves since 2014, with recent years counting more. On each one, it applies your lender's rules. The number at the top counts in how many of 100 futures your loan is liquidated. Two other methods check it.

One simulated future, step by step

The example loan: borrow €10,000 against BTC worth €20,000. Liquidation at LTV 80%, so at a fall of 37.5%.

  1. Pick 30 real days. A random start day since 2014; the next 30 days are taken as they happened: each day's move and its lowest price. Here, the first piece comes from March 2018.
  2. Join pieces. Twelve such pieces make a year. Crashes stay intact inside a piece, because bad days often follow bad days.
  3. Check every day. Is the day's lowest price below your liquidation price? Here it is, in month 6 (the crash piece from June 2022).
  4. Repeat 10,000 times, each time with other pieces.

Which volatility, and why it matters

The simulation does not use a volatility someone chose. It reuses real days, so it has the volatility of BTC's real history. But BTC has become calmer as its market grew:

Why recent years count more: as BTC's market grew, its swings shrank, from 80–90% a year in 2017–2021 to 42–52% since 2023. A larger market needs more money to move it by the same percentage. So the page gives recent years more weight, while older crashes still count: 2014–2018 still makes up 12% of all draws.

From 10,000 futures to your number

  • Counted: 5,270 of the 10,000 futures end with the loan liquidated: 52.7, shown as 53 of 100. Each dot stands for 100 futures.
  • Precise to about ±1: with 10,000 futures, the count would land within about one point if it were repeated with new random draws.
  • The same terms always give the same number, because the random draws are fixed. "Make it safer", the slider and the comparison of offers use the very same 10,000 futures, so a difference comes only from the one change.
  • Not a forecast: it says how often a loan like this failed in futures built from the past, not what will happen.
The details

Your lender's rules in the simulation

  • Every simulated day, the day's lowest price is compared with your liquidation price. That price rises when unpaid interest is added to the debt.
  • A margin call is checked at the day's closing price. The deadline is counted in whole days (48 hours = 2 days). The real-history method checks every hour.
  • Partial liquidations, fees and a cash reserve follow exactly the rules you set; the same code is used by all methods.

The test against history

From 2016 on, each month, the method saw only the past and predicted the lowest price of the next 90 and 365 days. A fair method is breached about 5 times in 100 at its "worst 5%" level. The simulation was breached 0 to 2 times: cautious rather than optimistic.

What the model cannot do

  • It only knows price moves that happened since 2014.
  • The weighting of recent years is a judgement; a return to 2017-like swings would make the true risk higher.
  • It steps day by day: a deadline of 30 hours counts as 2 days, and two crashes in one day count as one.
  • It joins 30-day pieces at random, so longer phases are mixed up.
  • It assumes no price trend.

The maths

Data

rt = ln(Ct / Ct−1),   ℓt = ln(min(Lt, Ct) / Ct−1)    (C: daily close, L: lowest hourly price of the day)

Simulation: weighted block bootstrap

block start s drawn with P(s) ∝ ws,   ws = 2−(n−1−s)/(3·365),   blocks of 30 days
no trend: r̃t = rt − r̄w,   r̄w = Σ wt rt / Σ wt    Sk = S0 · exp(Σj≤k r̃j)
liquidated on day k if   Sk−1 · eℓ̃k ≤ Dk−1 / (B · LTVliq)    (D: debt, B: BTC pledged)
p̂ = k / N,   N = 10,000,   standard error √(p̂(1−p̂)/N) ≈ 0.5 points at p̂ = 0.53

Volatility model: GARCH(1,1) with Student-t days

rt = μ + εt,   εt = √ht · zt,   zt ~ tν (scaled),   ht+1 = ω + α εt2 + β ht

Fitted by maximum likelihood on all days since 2014: α = 0.125, β = 0.875, ν = 3.2; today's volatility √(365·h) = 39% a year. The day's low is drawn with a Brownian bridge. (Values are computed at every build.)

Textbook formula

P(hit) = Φ((ln(b/S0) − νT)/(σ√T)) + (b/S0)2ν/σ² · Φ((ln(b/S0) + νT)/(σ√T)),   ν = μ − σ²/2 = 0
